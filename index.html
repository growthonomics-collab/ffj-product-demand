Portfolio matrix

Where every product sits, BCG style

The classic growth-share matrix, filled with our own two numbers. Share = the Popularity Ratio in the product's main hub (1.00 = hub average). Growth = momentum, the 90-day ratio divided by the 12-month ratio (1.00 = holding its position). Newcomers with no 12-month history are question marks by definition.

The formula
Demand Share % = 100 × [ 0.35 × Views ÷ ΣViews(hub) + 0.25 × Users ÷ ΣUsers(hub) + 0.15 × Engaged sessions ÷ ΣEngaged(hub) + 0.15 × Organic clicks ÷ ΣClicks(hub) + 0.10 × Add-to-cart ÷ ΣATC(hub) ]
Views and Users are recency-weighted: yesterday × 1.0, 30 days ago × 0.5, 60 days ago × 0.25 (30-day half-life)
Popularity Ratio = Demand Share % × Products in hub ÷ 100
Newcomers (under 90 days): Ratio = (real views × own ratio + 100 × 1.50) ÷ (real views + 100)
12-month check = same share on 12 months of GA4 (0.45 Views + 0.35 Users + 0.20 Engaged sessions); Trend = 90-day ratio ÷ 12-month ratio

Each product is scored against the other products actually listed on the same hub page (membership read from the site's own category listings, 365 pages; the remaining pages use the URL words). Ratio 1.00 = an average product of that hub; 3.00 and above = Hero (featured), 1.50 to 2.99 = Strong (first rows), 0.50 to 1.49 = Average (default order), under 0.50 = Low (back of the grid). Engaged sessions carry the engagement rate into the score (engaged sessions = sessions × engagement rate), so a page that people bounce from earns less than one they stay on. The 12-month column shows whether the 90-day position is new or established: Rising = 90-day ratio at least 25% above the 12-month ratio, Fading = at least 20% below. Sources: GA4 Pages and screens (90 days and 12 months), GA4 Ecommerce items, Search Console Pages.

Where a product stands when it enters after the formula
  1. Day 1: ratio 1.50, label New. With no views the prior is all there is: first rows of its hub, no Hero slot, no risk of the back of the grid.
  2. Every day after: the prior fades as views arrive. Its signals are scaled to a full window and blended with the prior in proportion to its real views: at 100 views it is halfway to its own performance, at 1,000 views the prior is gone. A strong launch shows in days, a weak one drifts down gently. No cliffs at day 30 or 90.
  3. Day 90 onwards: the plain formula. Launch date comes from GA4 (first day the URL had a view, with no earlier history); in the automated pipeline it comes from the CMS publish date.

Variations: what shoppers pick on the product page

How this feeds the ranking. The formula

Every product is scored inside its hub, never against the whole site, so a strong wedding band is not buried under engagement rings. The score is a Demand Share: the percentage of the hub's total demand that one product captures. Shares inside a hub add up to 100.

Demand Share (product, hub) =
  0.35 × Views(product) / Σ Views(hub)
 + 0.25 × Users(product) / Σ Users(hub)
 + 0.15 × Engaged sessions(product) / Σ Engaged sessions(hub)
 + 0.15 × Organic clicks(product) / Σ Organic clicks(hub)
 + 0.10 × Add-to-cart(product) / Σ Add-to-cart(hub)
 × 100

Engaged sessions = Sessions × Engagement rate (GA4: a session over 10 seconds, with a key event, or with 2+ page views)
SignalSourceWeightWhy
Page viewsGA4 · Pages and screens35%Raw interest. Counts repeat looks, which is how people shop for rings.
Active usersGA4 · Pages and screens25%Breadth of interest. Stops one visitor refreshing a page from inflating it.
Engaged sessionsGA4 · Pages and screens (Engagement rate × Sessions)15%Quality of interest. A product people stay on, scroll and click earns more than one they bounce from. Engagement rate alone is a percentage and cannot be shared out, so it enters as engaged sessions.
Organic clicksSearch Console · Pages15%Demand from Google, independent of where the site links internally.
Add-to-cartGA4 · Ecommerce purchases (items)10%Purchase intent. Low weight because tracking is uneven across product types.

If a hub has no data for a signal (for example no organic clicks yet), that weight is dropped and the others are rescaled, so the shares still add up to 100.

Reading the number

A Demand Share of 6.5 means that product captures 6.5% of everything the hub's shoppers do. Inside a hub of 470 oval rings the average product sits at 0.2, so anything above 2 is a hero piece. Compare shares within a hub only; a 30% share in the 37-product bracelet range is not the same as 30% in engagement rings.

Purchases are shown but not scored: 143 purchases across 6,352 items in 90 days is too sparse to rank on. Use them as a tie-break and as a check that a popular product also sells.

Engagement rate on its own

The tables also show each product's engagement rate. Site-wide, product pages run at about engaged sessions, against for the site as a whole. Read it as a quality check next to the ratio: a Hero product with an engagement rate well under the hub average is being clicked on but not liked (price, imagery or a mismatch with the listing it came from), and a Low product with a very high engagement rate is a candidate for better placement.

Variations from the product page

GA4's standard "Page path" drops everything after the question mark, so the first versions of this score treated a setting as one product however it was opened. The site writes the shopper's choices into the address (?shape=oval, attribute_pa_claws=round, attribute_pa_band-type=pave, attribute_pa_center-stone=lab, attribute_pa_metal-purity=9k), so a "Page path + query string" export recovers them: 604,680 of the period's product views carry a choice, on 2,992 products. Two uses follow. Shape pages are scored on shape-specific views (see the Variations tab), and every product shows the split of shapes, claws and bands its shoppers picked, which is the brief for imagery and for which variant to feature first.

Four upgrades over the first version

Hub membership from the site, not from names. Each of the 365 category pages was crawled for the products it actually lists. A product is ranked inside the pages that show it, and the "Not listed anywhere" filter exposes products with traffic that no hub page carries. The URL-word method remains only for builder and landing pages that have no fixed list.

Recency. Views and users are weighted with a 30-day half-life, so a product rising this month shows now rather than at the next quarter. The 12-month column still shows the long-run position.

A smooth start for newcomers. Instead of a fixed 1.50 for 30 days and a jump at day 30, a newcomer's ratio is a blend of its own scaled performance and the 1.50 prior, weighted by how many real views it has: (views × own ratio + 100 × 1.50) ÷ (views + 100).

Automation. The same score runs as a weekly job on the GA4 and Search Console APIs and writes the ratio, tier, trend and age into WooCommerce. The pipeline, its setup and a tracking spec for hub-level click data are in the pipeline folder delivered with this page.

How much history there is, and why 90 days

GA4 on this property goes back to at least January 2023, so there is no shortage of data. The reason the score runs on 90 days is the site itself: the current URL structure (engagement-rings/oval, diamond-rings/3-carat and so on) went live at the start of 2026, and before that the category pages sat under /product-category/. Category-page traffic before the switch does not map to today's hubs. Product URLs (/product/slug/) carried over, so for products a longer window is usable.

That is what the 12-month column does: the same share formula over 12 months of GA4 (12 September 2025 to 9 September 2026) using views, users and engaged sessions. Search Console and add-to-cart are not part of it, because the Search Console export only covers the current period and add-to-cart tracking changed during the year. The Trend flag compares the two: Rising means the 90-day ratio is at least 25% above the 12-month ratio (recent momentum, often a newer piece or one that gained links), Fading means at least 20% below (an established piece losing ground), Steady is anything between, New means it has 90-day data but no earlier history.

Autumn and winter figures are also worth remembering when reading the 12-month column: the 12 months include the Christmas and Valentine's peaks, the 90 days do not, so a seasonal piece can look like it is fading in September when it is simply between seasons.

How products are placed in hubs

GA4 has no item category or item list data for this property (both come through as "not set"), so hubs are built from the product URL and name. Each product is tagged with type, shape, metal, style, stone, diamond colour and carat, and a hub is simply a filter on those tags that mirrors the live URL structure (for example /engagement-rings/oval/ is every engagement ring tagged Oval). Named settings without attributes in the name ("Chelsea Yellow Gold Engagement Ring") only appear in hubs they can be tagged for, so they show under Engagement rings and Yellow gold but not under a shape.

Sources and coverage

    Search Console covers the top 1,000 pages by clicks in its export, so long-tail products carry organic clicks of zero. Views and users come from the full GA4 export (8,206 rows), so every product is scored.

    Refreshing the data

    1. GA4 → Reports → Engagement → Pages and screens. Customise the report (pencil icon) and add Engagement rate, Engaged sessions and Sessions to the metrics, Apply. Set the date range to the last 90 days, rows per page 250, then Share → Download CSV. Repeat with the last 12 months for the trend column. The export contains every row, not only the page shown.
    2. GA4 → Reports → Monetization → Ecommerce purchases (Item name). Same date range, Share → Download CSV.
    3. Search Console → Performance → Search results → Pages tab, same date range, Export → Download Excel.
    4. Run the scoring script on the three files. It rebuilds the hub tags, recomputes Demand Share and regenerates this page.

    When a new product enters the website

    A product that goes live after the formula has run has no history, so a plain share-of-hub score would give it close to 0 and push it to the back of its hub. That is the wrong signal: no data means unknown, not unpopular. The scoring now handles this itself. It reads each product's launch date from GA4 (the first day its URL had a view, counted only when there is no earlier history in the 12-month export) and applies the three-stage rule below before ranking. Nothing needs to be entered by hand; a product that appears between refreshes simply gets the provisional values in the CMS until the next run picks it up.

    The cold-start rule (built into the score)

    Age on siteRatio usedWhere it goes
    0 to 30 days1.50 (provisional)Strong tier: first rows of its hub, "New" label, never a Hero slot or the popular badge.
    30 to 89 daysScored, scaledIts views, users, engaged sessions, clicks and add-to-carts are multiplied by 90 ÷ days live, then it competes normally. With under 100 real views the sample is too small: it stays at 1.50 and New.
    90 days and overScored normallySame rules as every other product from the next quarterly refresh onwards.

    Launch checklist

    1. Tag it on day one. Shape, metal, style, stone, colour and carat in the product name and URL slug, the same words the hubs use (oval, yellow-gold, cluster, lab-grown, pink, 2-00-carat). The scoring reads these to place the product in its sub-hubs; a name like "Aurora Ring" lands in the main hub only.
    2. Set the provisional ratio of 1.50 and the "New" flag in the CMS so the hub sort picks it up immediately.
    3. Give it two internal links from the hub it belongs to (intro copy or a "New in" block) so it collects organic clicks from the start. Organic clicks are 15% of the score and the slowest signal to build.
    4. Add it to the Search Console watch list. If it has impressions but no clicks after 30 days, the title and image need work before the score is trusted.
    5. At day 30 run the score. Hero or Strong: move it into the earned slot and drop the "New" label. Average or Low: it keeps the default position, and the merchandising question becomes price, imagery or duplication with an existing best seller.

    Two edge cases

    A relaunch or rename keeps its history: match on the old URL for 90 days so a redirect does not reset a Hero product to provisional. A seasonal piece (Valentine's, Christmas) gets the provisional ratio from the day it goes live each season, then scores on that season's numbers only.

    Using it on the website

    Three concrete uses, in order of effort.

    1. Hub page ordering. Put the top five Demand Share products first on each hub page and in its "popular" block. Revisit quarterly.
    2. Internal linking. From each hub page, link to its top three products in the intro copy, and from those products back to the hub. This is the fastest way to move organic clicks onto pieces that already convert attention.
    3. Merchandising gaps. Where the Category hubs tab shows a hub with strong page views but its products carry low Demand Share, the hub is sending traffic to pieces people do not want to look at. Refresh the selection there first.
